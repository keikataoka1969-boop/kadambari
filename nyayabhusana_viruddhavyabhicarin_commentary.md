# Nyāyabhūṣaṇa on Nigamana and Viruddhāvyabhicārin

## A Cross-Textual Commentary

---

## 1. Introduction

The Nyāyabhūṣaṇa (न्यायभूषण) of Bhāsarvajña presents one of the most sophisticated defenses of the *nigamana* (conclusion) as a necessary member (*avayava*) of the five-membered syllogism (*pañcāvayava*). This defense is intimately connected with the problem of *viruddhāvyabhicārin*—the "contradictorily non-deviating" pseudo-reason (*hetvābhāsa*)—and with the broader question of what constitutes a valid inferential proof.

This commentary examines the Nyāyabhūṣaṇa's arguments in dialogue with parallel discussions in Jayanta Bhaṭṭa's Nyāyamañjarī, the Nyāyapraveśa tradition, and related texts.

---

## 2. The Nyāyabhūṣaṇa on the Purpose of Nigamana

### 2.1 Rejection of the "Sentence-Completion" View

The Nyāyabhūṣaṇa opens its discussion with a *pūrvapakṣa*:

> *athedaṃ kiṃ prayojanam iti? vākyaparisamāptyartham ity eke.*
>
> "Now, what is the purpose [of nigamana]? Some say: for the completion of the sentence."

Bhāsarvajña rejects this immediately:

> *tad ayuktam; tatsamāptau prayojanābhāvāt.*
>
> "That is wrong, because there is no purpose in such completion."

The argument is that in inference (*anumāna*), the goal is to establish an object (*artha*) through the invariable concomitance (*avinābhāva*) of the mark (*liṅga*). Once the inseparable mark is stated, the *sādhya* (probandum) follows of its own accord (*svayam evāvagacchatīti*). If so, why add a redundant concluding statement?

### 2.2 The Objection from Parallel Cases

An objector raises:

> *gamyamānārthasyānabhidhāne nidarśanāder apy anabhidhānaprasaṅga iti cet*
>
> "If what is already understood need not be stated, then the example (*nidarśana*) too should not be stated."

Bhāsarvajña's response is striking:

> *bhavatu, yadi tadantareṇāpi sādhyasiddhir bhavati. na hi vyasanam evaitad asya yat siddhe 'py arthe vaśyaṃ vākyaṃ pūrayitavyam iti.*
>
> "So be it, if the probandum is established even without it. For it is no compulsion that one must fill out a sentence even for an already established object."

This shows Bhāsarvajña's pragmatic approach: the members of a syllogism are not fixed by formal requirements of "sentence completeness" but by their epistemic function.

### 2.3 The Positive Function: Indicating the Absence of Opposing Proofs

The pivotal argument comes next:

> *nedam anarthakaṃ sādhyaviruddhābhāvapratipādakapramāṇasūcakatvād asya*
>
> "This [nigamana] is not purposeless, because it serves to indicate (*sūcaka*) the means of knowledge (*pramāṇa*) that establishes the absence of what contradicts the probandum."

This is Bhāsarvajña's distinctive contribution. The *nigamana* is not mere repetition—it functions as a signal (*sūcaka*) that the inferential reason is free from the defect of *satpratipakṣa* (having a real counter-proof). The argument unfolds:

> *asatpratipakṣatvāpratipādane 'py avinābhāvo pratipādita eva syāt pakṣadharmatvādivad asatpratipakṣatvasyāpi hetūrūpatvāt tatpratipādanārthaṃ ca nigamanam.*
>
> "Even without establishing the absence of a counter-proof, the invariable concomitance would be established. But since the absence of a counter-proof (*asatpratipakṣatva*) is itself a form of the reason (*hetūrūpatva*), just like being a property of the thesis (*pakṣadharmatva*), the nigamana serves to establish it."

### 2.4 The Illustration: "Therefore Impermanent"

Bhāsarvajña provides a concrete example:

> *tasmād anitya iti saty ārambho niyamaṃ gamayaty anitya eva tasmān na nitya iti sādhyaviruddhasya pratipakṣākhyasyāsattvaṃ gamyate*
>
> "'Therefore [it is] impermanent'—when there is a productive cause, it conveys the restriction: impermanent *only*, therefore not permanent—thus the non-existence of the counter-thesis (*pratipakṣa*), which contradicts the probandum, is understood."

And from this follows:

> *tataś ca nāyaṃ viruddhāvyabhicārī na prakaraṇasama iti siddhaṃ bhavati.*
>
> "And from this it is established that [the reason] is neither a *viruddhāvyabhicārin* nor a *prakaraṇasama*."

This passage is crucial: the *nigamana* serves to *rule out* the possibility that the reason falls into the class of *viruddhāvyabhicārin* or *prakaraṇasama* pseudo-reasons.

## 3. The Regress Argument (*Anavasthā*)

A powerful dialectical move follows. The opponent objects that to establish the absence of a counter-proof, one needs a separate *pramāṇa* (means of valid knowledge), not mere verbal assertion (*vacanamātra*):

> *nanu pratipakṣābhāvasiddhau pramāṇāntaraṃ vācyaṃ na nigamanākhyaṃ vacanamātram*
>
> "But for establishing the absence of a counter-proof, another means of knowledge should be stated, not a mere utterance called nigamana."

Bhāsarvajña turns this against the opponent with a *tu quoque*:

> *evaṃ tarhi pakṣadharmatvādisiddhāv api pramāṇāntaram eva vācyam na hetūdāharaṇādivacanamātram*
>
> "In that case, even for establishing pakṣadharmatva etc., another means of knowledge should be stated, not the mere verbal statement of the reason and example."

And then the clinching response:

> *pakṣadharmatvādisādhanapramāṇastabakatvān na vacanamātram iti cet, nigamanam api tarhi pratipakṣābhāvasādhakapramāṇasūcakatvān na vacanamātram.*
>
> "If [you say] it is not mere verbal assertion because it is a cluster of pramāṇas establishing pakṣadharmatva etc., then the nigamana too is not mere verbal assertion, because it indicates the pramāṇa establishing the absence of a counter-proof."

The symmetry is perfect: if the *hetu* and *udāharaṇa* are not "mere words" because they point to pramāṇas, then the *nigamana* is equally not "mere words" because it too points to a pramāṇa.

Were the opponent to press further, demanding yet another pramāṇa for each component, the result is an infinite regress (*anavasthā*):

> *so 'pi na kartavyas tadarthe vipratipadyamāno yena pramāṇāntareṇa pratibodhayituṃ śakyas tad eva vaktavyam ity anavastheti.*
>
> "That [further pramāṇa] too need not be produced—if the opponent contests it, then whatever further pramāṇa could make them understand should be stated—and so on to infinity."

## 4. Nigamana and Bādhaka-pramāṇa

### 4.1 The Relationship between Nigamana and Indirect Proof

Bhāsarvajña connects the *nigamana* to *bādhaka*-pramāṇa (disproving counter-evidence):

> *yadi nigamanābhidhānam asādhanāṅgam abhyupagamyate, tataḥ kṣaṇikatvādisiddhau sattvakṛtakatvādisādhanam eva vaktavyam. asanto kṣaṇikās tasyāṃ kramākramavirodhataḥ ityādinā (vipakṣa)bādhakaṃ pramāṇaṃ nābhyupagantavyam.*
>
> "If the statement of nigamana is accepted as not being a component of proof, then for establishing momentariness etc., only the proof through existence and being-produced should be stated. The disproving pramāṇa beginning with 'Non-existent [things] are momentary, because of the contradiction of sequence and simultaneity' should not be accepted."

This is directed at Buddhist logicians: if you deny that the *nigamana* serves a function, then your own *bādhaka*-pramāṇas (which serve the same function of ruling out counter-proofs) should also be rejected. The reason:

> *kasmān nigamanārthatvād bādhakasya*
>
> "Because the bādhaka [pramāṇa] serves the purpose of the nigamana."

Both the *nigamana* and the *bādhaka*-pramāṇa accomplish the same thing: *viparītaprasaṅgapratiṣedha*—the negation of the undesirable consequence—by ruling out the counter-thesis through demonstrating the invariable concomitance of the reason.

### 4.2 The Pragmatic Conclusion

> *tasmād yadaiva yad avayavārthaṃ prati vipratipadyate paras tadaiva tad avayavārthasamarthanārthaṃ bādhakaṃ sādhakaṃ vā pramāṇāntaram ucyata ity alaṃ prasaṅgena.*
>
> "Therefore, whenever the opponent contests the meaning of some component [of the syllogism], precisely then a disproving or proving pramāṇa is cited in support of that component's meaning. Enough of this digression."

The conclusion is pragmatic: each *avayava* has its place; supplementary proofs (*bādhaka*, *sādhaka*) are deployed only as needed when challenged.

## 5. Viruddhāvyabhicārin: Cross-Textual Perspectives

### 5.1 Jayanta Bhaṭṭa's Nyāyamañjarī: Rejection of Viruddhāvyabhicārin

Jayanta takes a more radical position than Bhāsarvajña. In the Nyāyamañjarī (NM 2.608–618, and the verse at NM 1.316.08), he argues that *viruddhāvyabhicārin* is not a genuine *hetvābhāsa* at all:

> *viruddhāvyabhicāro vā sarvatra sulabhodayaḥ* (NM 1.316.08, v. 205)
>
> "Or, the viruddhāvyabhicāra has an easy occurrence everywhere."

His key arguments (drawn from NM2 and NMsarit.tex):

1. **Impossibility of two contradictory proofs applying to a single locus** (*ekatra dharmiṇi parasparaviruddhadharmadvayākṣepiprayojakahetudvayasannipātāyogāt*): Two reasons producing contradictory properties in one subject cannot co-occur, because things cannot have dual natures (*na hi dvyātmakāni vastūni bhavitum arhanti*).

2. **Not a genuine *anaikāntika***: The *viruddhāvyabhicārin* does not fall under the definition of *anaikāntika* (inconclusive reason) because it does not occur in both the *sapakṣa* and *vipakṣa* (*pakṣadvayavṛttyabhāvāt*). Jayanta's definition of *anaikāntika* is *pakṣadvayavṛttitva* (occurrence in both thesis-classes), and the *viruddhāvyabhicārin* does not satisfy this.

3. **Classification as *jāti* (sophistical rejoinder)**: Rather than being a *hetvābhāsa*, the *viruddhāvyabhicārin* is merely a type of sophistical objection (*jātiprayoga*). It belongs among the unlimited types of fallacious rejoinders, not among the defective reasons proper.

4. **Dharmmakīrti's rejection**: Jayanta notes that while earlier Buddhist logicians like Dignāga accepted the *viruddhāvyabhicārin* as producing doubt (*saṃśāyakatayā*), Dharmakīrti and later Buddhists rejected it (*dharmakīrtyādibhir eva tannirāsāt*).

### 5.2 The Nyāyabhūṣaṇa's Prakaraṇasama Connection

The Nyāyabhūṣaṇa passage (NBhu2, l. 2051) connects *viruddhāvyabhicārin* with *prakaraṇasama*:

> *ubhayavaidharmyāt prakriyāsiddheḥ prakaraṇasama iti. asyāpi sādharmyavaidharmyasamābhyām uktimātreṇa bhedaḥ*
>
> "The prakaraṇasama [is so called] because the procedure is established through dissimilarity with both. The difference from this [viruddhāvyabhicārin] too is merely in whether [the counter-argument is framed] through similarity or dissimilarity."

And the crucial distinction:

> *yadi tu viruddhāvyabhicārivat trirūpeṇa sādharmyeṇa pratyavasthānaṃ prakaraṇasamaḥ, sādharmyasamas tu sādharmyamātreṇa pratyavasthānam ity ucyate; tadaitad vailakṣaṇyam apy asti.*
>
> "If, like the viruddhāvyabhicārin, the counter-argument through the three-formed positive similarity is called prakaraṇasama, while [one made] through mere positive similarity is called sādharmyasama—then this distinction too exists."

### 5.3 The Nayakalikā (Jayanta): A Concise Summary

In the Nayakalikā (Nkalika.tex, ll. 270–276), Jayanta gives his most compressed statement:

> *viruddhāvyabhicārī tu nāma hetvābhāso nāsty eva. ekatra dharmiṇi viruddhadharmasādhanayoḥ dvayor hetvor anupanipātāt.*
>
> "There simply is no hetvābhāsa called viruddhāvyabhicārin, because two reasons establishing contradictory properties cannot co-occur in a single subject."

And:

> *na cāyam eva viruddhāvyabhicārī bhavati. anaikāntikalakṣaṇābhāvād iti.*
>
> "Nor is this [so-called] viruddhāvyabhicārin [truly] such. Because it lacks the defining mark of the anaikāntika."

### 5.4 The Rāmānuja Tradition

The passage from Epic\newram3.DN (ll. 351, 353) shows the concept deployed in Vaiṣṇava theology:

> *nāpy anīśvarakartṛkaṃ jagat kāryatvāt ghaṭādivad iti viruddhāvyabhicāryākrāntatvād ahetūr ayam*
>
> "Nor [can it be argued that] the world is not made by God because it is an effect like a pot, etc.—this is a non-reason because it is afflicted by viruddhāvyabhicāra."

The author then argues that if such pseudo-reasons were universally applicable, inference itself would collapse:

> *anyathā parvatādāv api dhūmād agny-anumāne... sarvatra viruddhāvyabhicāry-anumānāvaskarasaṃbhavād anumānābhāva eva*
>
> "Otherwise, even in the inference of fire from smoke on a mountain... since the material for a viruddhāvyabhicārin-inference is available everywhere, there would be no inference at all."

## 6. Philosophical Synthesis

### 6.1 Bhāsarvajña's Position in Context

Bhāsarvajña occupies a middle ground between two extremes:

- **The Buddhist (Dignāga-Dharmakīrti) tradition**: Originally recognized *viruddhāvyabhicārin* as a distinct type of pseudo-reason producing doubt, but Dharmakīrti's followers eventually rejected it.
- **Jayanta Bhaṭṭa**: Denies it is a *hetvābhāsa* altogether, classifying it as a *jāti* (sophistical rejoinder).

Bhāsarvajña's innovation is to connect the problem to the *nigamana*. His argument runs:

1. The *nigamana* is not a mere verbal formality.
2. It serves to indicate the pramāṇa that establishes the absence of counter-proofs (*sādhyaviruddhābhāvapratipādakapramāṇasūcakatva*).
3. Through this function, the *nigamana* demonstrates that the reason is free from *viruddhāvyabhicāra* and *prakaraṇasama* defects.
4. This function parallels the *bādhaka*-pramāṇa used by Buddhist logicians themselves.

### 6.2 The Structural Argument

The deeper structural argument is about **what makes an inference complete**. For Bhāsarvajña:

- Stating the *hetu* (reason) with its *avinābhāva* (invariable concomitance) establishes the *positive* case.
- The *nigamana* establishes the *negative* case: it rules out that the reason is a *viruddhāvyabhicārin* or a *prakaraṇasama*.
- Without the *nigamana*, an inference might establish the *sādhya* but leave open the logical possibility of a counter-proof of equal strength.

This is structurally analogous to modern notions of "closing the proof"—not just showing that the conclusion follows, but that no equally valid counter-argument undermines it.

### 6.3 The Regress and Its Resolution

The regress argument (*anavasthā*) is Bhāsarvajña's most powerful dialectical tool. It shows that the demand for ever-higher-order pramāṇas to validate each component of the syllogism is self-defeating. Every *avayava* points to a pramāṇa; the verbal statement (*vacana*) is the *sūcaka* (indicator), not the *pramāṇa* itself. Once this is understood, the *nigamana* takes its proper place as a necessary indicator—on par with, not subordinate to, the *hetu* and *udāharaṇa*.

## 8. Key Sanskrit Terms

- **viruddhāvyabhicārin** (विरुद्धाव्यभिचारिन्): A pseudo-reason (hetvābhāsa) where two contradictory reasons, each individually valid, apply to the same subject, producing doubt rather than certainty.
- **nigamana** (निगमन): The conclusion; the fifth member of the Nyāya five-part syllogism (*pañcāvayava*).
- **sādhyaviruddhābhāvapratipādakapramāṇasūcakatva**: "The property of indicating the pramāṇa that establishes the absence of what contradicts the probandum"—Bhāsarvajña's definition of nigamana's function.
- **prakaraṇasama** (प्रकरणसम): A pseudo-reason where the counter-argument has equal strength to the original argument.
- **satpratipakṣa** (सत्प्रतिपक्ष): "Having a real counter-proof"—a genuine defect of a reason.
- **bādhaka-pramāṇa** (बाधकप्रमाण): A means of valid knowledge that disproves a counter-thesis.
- **anavasthā** (अनवस्था): Infinite regress.
- **jāti** (जाति): A sophistical or futile rejoinder in debate.
- **avinābhāva** (अविनाभाव): Invariable concomitance; the necessary co-occurrence of the reason with the probandum.
